## What the keys cost and why the posterior differs

Across each fit, checking entire native curved operands took **182.45 ms dense and 165.97 ms hard**. Checking predictive covariance inputs took **45.01 and 33.97 ms**. The additional initial common-design removal and comparison took **74.98 and 58.31 ms**. The underlying dictionary lookup portion of all these closely related counters remained below a millisecond per pair. Recording-scoped interpolation keys were cheaper, costing about **3.13 and 2.85 ms** including one-time bank fingerprinting, the observed lookups and rechecking per-row effective phase. Separately, frozen bank and row validation cost **2.60 and 2.32 ms**. The whole audit spent **316.06 and 269.69 ms** computing keys before final validation, so naïve byte hashing is already much dearer than the measured opportunity. This does not price a hypothetical specialized C lookup or mutable production cache.

Across the four common positions, both arms used the same response, point sigmas, group labels, powers, and common columns in the selected design. Fitted calibration adds one RF column and changes the matrix shape. The zero-reference operands can share some fixed-weight products. The actual fully evaluated likelihood includes fitted RF and arm-specific posterior coefficients and covariances, feeding its next noise and prior updates. The four peers share zero byte-identical final precision vectors. Every later curved phase view has a distinct effective interpolation key. The downstream predictive covariance inputs all differ as a result. Even eliminating **all** of the paid curved posterior work would recover only **0.450 s on dense and 0.321 s on hard**, including the two same-view summary repeats. That gives scale to a possible future redesign of the initial linear algebra. It does not credit reuse of either arm's robust factor.
