# Passive spectrum transfer check

The experiment asks whether a stationary receiver can recover geography from measured Ku-band frequency changes at a different physical installation. The input is one-second average power from the Watson station in the UK. The broad prior is a fixed 49.5–61.5° N, 9° W–3° E rectangle. The tile lattice partitions that rectangle into 0.25° by 0.375° cells, about 25–28 km across in this region. Every tile uses the same center-point budget. The inference takes no station coordinates, author transit predictions, or site-selected satellite lists. The scoring command reads the reference after the prediction lock. The author notes and earlier local field report identify the station publicly, so the experiment is algorithmically blind.

Every selected hour contributes its first 30 minutes. The dates and 06:00 and 18:00 UTC starts were selected before examining the measurements. March 2 has a different IF grid in both selected member headers. It becomes the recorded off-band control. March 16 replaces it for development. March 14 and March 23 remain held. The same archive hardware contributes every day. Earlier studies and an unsuccessful internal beacon-comb detector used March 11, which is excluded from this experiment.

An isolated spectral ridge must persist for at least 18 recorded seconds, show a physically plausible negative frequency change, and fit a smooth curve within the scan resolution. The extractor uses local and per-window median power subtraction. A track has no satellite name. Overlapping ridges at an approximate harmonic spacing count once when their frequency changes agree. Only causal Starlink TLEs enter the primary calculation. The same calculation with OneWeb ephemerides and with rotated ridge timestamps serves as a negative control. Geometry-only retains the real ridge timestamps and TLE visibility but drops their frequencies. No-detection runs use the same grid and catalogue. One separate calculation tests a north-directed angular extent from the reported fixed antenna orientation.

The clock offset candidates are −30, −20, −10, 0, 10, 20 and 30 seconds. The best shared offset and grid position minimize average development-window loss. A development window without ridges leaves the clock unresolved and fixes it at zero. Every held window uses the locked offset. The primary calculation requires satellite elevation above 5° on a ridge and compares changes in frequency. It selects one trial satellite on six evenly spaced ridge samples while fitting a constant frequency offset. Four later ridge samples supply a predictive residual anchored to that fit. A publishable estimate requires at least three distinct supported candidate identities, capped loss at most 0.50, and a 0.06 margin against the strongest alternative at least 60 km away. These thresholds measure evidence stability. A control can therefore produce an erroneous publication and remains part of the result.

The broad search considers every tile for every measurement and control. An offer can admit only the broad-search publication. For every eligible anchor cell, a fixed hash preselects the orientation of three decoys 4 grid cells from the anchor, about 100 km along either axis. The omitted offer replaces the anchor with a fourth distinct displaced cell. Selection of the applicable anchor waits for the geographic score. The location-free prior treats every offered cell equally. A forced comparison chooses the lowest loss in the four offers if their losses differ. The experiment covers archival power spectra. IQ and known-pilot acquisition require additional measurements. One nominal RF center leaves the RF-dependent calibration coefficient confounded with a constant frequency offset.
